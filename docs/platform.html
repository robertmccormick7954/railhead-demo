White-label storefronts

Your brand in front, one shared deployment behind it

An agency that licenses this platform gets a storefront of its own: its own domain, its own name and colours, its own prices, its own customers and its own legal identity on every page. What it does not get is a copy of the codebase to look after. Every difference between one agency's storefront and another's is a value in a configuration file, and there is no tenant-specific stylesheet or markup anywhere in the build.

What is white-labelled

One agency is one entry in a configuration document. Applying it writes custom properties onto the page and formats every time, date, distance and amount through the agency's locale. Nothing below requires a build, a branch or a separate deployment.

Three storefronts, one deployment, on this page

The header of this demonstration carries a storefront selector listing three agencies. Choose another and the page reloads with every item in the table above changed: colours, type, corner radius, mark and wordmark, clock, date order, distance unit, currency, payment methods, support block and the legal identity in the footer. The address of the page you are reading does not change, because there is only one of it. Nothing was rebuilt, and no second deployment exists.

The three demonstration agencies were given deliberately unlike settings, so the switch is a test rather than a colour swap: they differ in currency, in clock format, in date order, in distance unit, in corner radius and in the way their type is set.

Engineering answer

A badly chosen brand colour cannot produce an unreadable storefront

Agencies choose their own brand colours, and some of those colours are pale, some are mid-tone, and some were picked for a printed sign rather than a screen. Two colours are therefore never read from the configuration. They are computed from it, against the surface they will actually sit on, at the moment the agency's settings are applied and before the page is painted.

The first is the label on a filled control. It is set to black or white, whichever has the higher contrast ratio against the brand colour behind it, measured with the WCAG relative luminance formula. A pale brand colour gets dark button text automatically; a deep one gets white.

The second is brand-coloured text: headings, links and small brand-tinted labels on a card. A colour that is perfectly legible as a button fill is often too light to read as text, so this one is mixed toward black in twenty steps until it clears a 4.5:1 ratio against the surface it will be read on, and toward white instead when that surface is dark. Only the text token moves. Filled areas keep the exact colour the agency chose, so the brand still looks like itself while the words on top of it stay readable.

The practical consequence for an agency is that legibility is not something to review and approve. There is no combination of configured colours that produces button labels or link text below the target, because those two colours are not settings. WCAG 2.1 Level AA is the standard this build works to.

Pricing control

Pricing happens in two stages that never merge. The carrier fare is produced first, by the fare provider. The agency's markup is applied afterwards by its own pricing policy, and it stays visible as a separate amount from that point to the receipt.

The pricing controls an agency holds
Control What it does
Percentage markup A percentage of the carrier fare for the whole itinerary.
Fixed markup per passenger A flat amount added once for each fare-paying passenger, or once per room where the accommodation is priced per room rather than per person.
Minimum A floor. Where the percentage and the fixed amount together fall below it, the markup is raised to the floor, so a short cheap journey still covers the cost of selling it.
Maximum A cap. An expensive sleeper itinerary does not take a percentage-sized margin that is out of proportion to the work involved.
Per-route-category override A different percentage for a named category of route, so an agency can price a short day trip and a two-night sleeper differently without touching either default. Thruway coach connections, which are scheduled motorcoach legs inside a rail itinerary, can carry a category of their own.
Promotional codes A percentage or a fixed amount off the subtotal after markup, carrying its own label. A promotion never reduces the carrier fare, so it comes out of the agency's margin and nowhere else.
Charge label The wording the markup appears under in the customer's price breakdown. The agency writes it.

The order is fixed: percentage on the carrier fare, plus the fixed amount for each passenger or room, clamped to the floor and the cap, then any promotional code applied to the subtotal. What the customer sees is the carrier fare, the agency's charge on its own named line, any discount on its own line, and the total.

Showing the markup separately is a deliberate constraint rather than a display option. It means an agency cannot present its own margin as the carrier's price, a customer comparing two quotes can see exactly what differs between them, and a refund can be worked out line by line instead of by unpicking a single blended number.

The agency console

A separate area of the same deployment, for the agency's own staff rather than its customers. Six screens.

Dashboard

Bookings and takings for the period, split into carrier fare and agency margin so the two are never read as one figure, with recent activity and the status of the schedule data the storefront is searching.

Branding

Colours, typeface pairing, corner radius, mark and wordmark, edited against a live preview and exported as the same configuration structure the deployment reads, so what an agency approves on screen is the file that ships.

Pricing

Percentage and fixed markup, floor and cap, per-route-category overrides, promotional codes and the label the charge carries, each change priced against a sample itinerary before it is saved.

Bookings ledger

Every booking the storefront has taken, found by reservation reference, passenger name, route or date, each opening on the itinerary and the price breakdown exactly as the customer saw it.

Payments

What was collected, by method, and how each amount divides between carrier fare and agency margin, together with refunds and where each one has reached.

Settings

Locale and currency, clock format, date order, distance unit, the payment methods offered, the support details printed across the storefront and the legal identity shown in the footer.

The console in this demonstration runs against the same simulated bookings and payments as the storefront. No money moves through this build, and no reservation in it exists in any carrier's system.

What the platform does not do

Stated here rather than discovered during an integration, because each of these decides whether the platform fits an agency's business at all.

  • It holds no inventory of its own. There is no allocation, no block of seats, no negotiated rate sitting behind this software. Every seat sold is the carrier's, reached through whatever distribution channel the agency is contracted to, and the platform is the shopfront and the record, not the source.
  • It cannot service a booking the carrier issued elsewhere. A reservation made on the carrier's own website, at a station, over the telephone, or through a different agency cannot be retrieved, changed or refunded here. The platform can service what it sold through the channel it sold it on. A customer holding a booking from somewhere else has to go back to whoever sold it.
  • It requires a distribution agreement. Held either by the agency or by the platform operator, in a named party's name, before anything is sold. Amtrak publishes no public fare or booking API, so live inventory reaches a reseller only through such an agreement: directly with Amtrak, through the agent portal now operated by Distribusion Technologies, or through an aggregator such as SilverRail. There is no fourth route, and the fares in this demonstration are simulated behind a provider interface for exactly that reason.
  • It is not a payment provider. It never holds funds, is not the merchant of record and does not settle anything. It presents the methods the agency's own provider supports and hands the transaction to it.
  • It is not a carrier and not an agent of one. It does not run trains, publish fares, set conditions of carriage, or speak for any operator named on a storefront.

This build issues nothing. The schedules are the carrier's real published timetable, and everything transactional is simulated. No payment is taken, no reservation is created in any carrier's system, and no document produced here is valid for travel.

What an agency has to bring

The platform supplies the storefront, the booking flow, the console and the schedule data. Four things it cannot supply, and none of them can be added later without stopping sales.

  • A distribution agreement. This is the one that decides whether an agency can trade at all. Amtrak's ticket terms state that “Amtrak tickets may only be sold or issued by Amtrak or an authorized travel agent/tour operator”, and that a ticket bought from an unauthorised third party is voided, with the holder eligible for neither travel nor a refund. The agreement, and nothing on a website, is what makes a retailer authorised.
  • A payment provider. A merchant account in the agency's own name, able to accept the methods it wants to offer in the currency it sells in, and to process refunds and carry chargeback exposure. The platform integrates with it; the money is the agency's to take and to give back.
  • Its own legal and consumer-protection registrations. The legal entity that appears on the receipt, whatever seller-of-travel or travel-guarantee registration the markets it sells into require, and its own terms of sale and privacy notice. The storefront prints these and keeps them in front of the customer at the right moments; it does not supply them, and no registration is implied by the software.
  • Support staff. The agency is the party the customer is buying from and the party they will contact, and the opening hours printed on the storefront are the agency's own. Somebody has to be reachable during them, able to make changes and refunds within the carrier's rules, and able to handle the accessibility and station-assistance requests the booking flow accepts rather than passing them on.
