How it connects

Written for the person who has to decide whether this is competent. It states what is real, what is simulated, and exactly what would have to be true for it to sell a ticket.

The schedule import

The feed is a public ZIP. It is fetched, parsed and compiled to two JSON files that the browser loads directly — no API sits between them. The compiled network is small enough that a full year of timetable arrives as part of the page.

Two defects we correct on import

The feed is not clean, and a booking engine that trusts it prints wrong times. Both problems were found by cross-checking against the carrier's own live train feed, which states a real UTC offset for every scheduled call.

Wrong timezone on twelve stations. Seven Arizona stations are labelled America/Denver. Arizona does not observe daylight saving, so for eight months of the year that is an hour wrong. Five Gulf Coast stations in Mississippi and Alabama are labelled America/New_York when they are Central.

Two clocks inside one trip. The feed writes stop times in Eastern throughout — except on the Gulf Coast service, where five stops carry local Central times while the New Orleans stop on the same train carries Eastern. The import detects the whole-hour discrepancy from observed offsets and normalises those stops, then refuses to build if the correction would reorder a trip.

Both corrections are recorded in the build output rather than applied silently, and the whole conversion chain is re-checked against the live feed on every build.

Distances

The feed carries no distance along a trip, so segment mileage is computed between consecutive stops and scaled by a track-sinuosity factor calibrated against fifteen published city-pair mileages. It reproduces the published figures closely — New Orleans to Los Angeles computes to within a mile of the published 1,995.

Why fares are simulated

Not a shortcut. There is no public route to Amtrak fares, and every alternative to a distribution agreement is closed:

  • There is no public fare or booking API. The one credentialed endpoint that exists is not publicly documented or obtainable.
  • The GTFS feed contains no fare data at all — no fare attributes, no fare rules, no fare products.
  • Amtrak's Terms of Use prohibit using “any robot, spider, site search/retrieval application or other manual or automatic device or process to retrieve, index, ‘data mine’ or in any way reproduce or circumvent the navigational structure or presentation of the Site or its contents”, and separately prohibit republishing its content.
  • Its robots file disallows exactly the paths its own booking front end calls.
  • Its edge blocks non-residential traffic outright, so a server-side scrape fails at the network layer before it reaches the legal problem.

There is also a consumer-protection reason that matters more than any of those. Amtrak's ticket conditions state that it may refuse to carry passengers “Who present an Amtrak ticket purchased from an unauthorized third party. Amtrak tickets may only be sold or issued by Amtrak or an authorized travel agent/tour operator. Any ticket purchased from an unauthorized third party will be voided. The ticket holder will not be eligible for travel or for a refund.” A reseller without an agreement does not merely breach a contract — it sells documents that leave the passenger stranded and unrefunded.

So the demonstration shows the seam rather than faking the connection. Prices come from a provider object with one method. The build ships a simulated implementation, clearly labelled everywhere it surfaces, and a production deployment registers a different one.

The provider interface

unit Per person, per room, or per vehicle — rooms and vehicles are not priced per traveller.

Getting real inventory

Three routes exist. All three are commercial before they are technical: the gate is the agreement, not the integration.

Route 1

Direct with the carrier

A distribution agreement with Amtrak, giving access to its partner API. Amtrak publishes no public criteria for granting one, and it segments travel professionals by the channel they already belong to.

Route 2

Through the agent portal

Amtrak's agent estate migrated off its long-standing portal; the replacement is built and operated by Distribusion Technologies, which states it integrated Amtrak's API and which sells API and white-label access commercially.

Route 3

Through an aggregator

A rail aggregator that already holds carrier agreements, in exchange for a share of the margin and less control over the retail experience.

Two limits worth stating before anyone asks.

A retailer can service only the tickets its own booking channel issued. Exchanges, refunds and seat changes on a ticket the carrier issued elsewhere belong to the carrier, and this product hands off rather than pretending otherwise.

Group travel above the carrier's online threshold is a request channel, not a transaction. This build captures a structured enquiry and routes it; it does not claim to confirm group space instantly.

How the build is put together

No runtime dependencies

No framework, no bundler, no package manager, no third-party script. The pages are static HTML with ES modules. The QR encoder and the PDF writer are written out rather than imported, because a ticket has to work on a phone with no signal.

Self-hosted fonts

The three typefaces are served from this origin. A white-label product runs on the agency's domain, and a third-party font request would put an external call — and a visitor IP disclosure — inside someone else's brand.

One deployment, many storefronts

A tenant is a configuration object. Applying it writes CSS custom properties; nothing downstream hard-codes a colour or a typeface. The two colours that carry text are computed from the agency's own brand colour against the surface they sit on, so a badly chosen palette cannot produce an unreadable storefront.

What is tested, and how

The timezone conversion is checked against the carrier's live feed on every build: every station-local departure time our code prints must equal the wall-clock time the live feed states for the same call.

The QR encoder is checked two ways — module-for-module against a reference implementation, and by decoding the rendered image back to the original payload.

The PDF writer's font metrics are checked against the published Adobe values, because wrong metrics show up as quietly truncated text on a printed ticket rather than as an error.

Contrast is verified programmatically across the interface, compositing translucent layers rather than reading declared colours.
