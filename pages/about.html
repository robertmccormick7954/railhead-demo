Disclosure

Who sells the ticket, and who runs the train

Two separate companies stand behind any rail journey bought from a retailer. The travel agency whose name is at the top of this page sells the ticket. Amtrak operates the train. They are not the same organisation and they are not connected, and this page exists so that nobody has to work that out for themselves.

Three parties, three jobs

A white-label storefront has one more party in it than most people expect, so all three are named here rather than left implied.

Stated without hedging: this storefront and the platform behind it are not affiliated with Amtrak, not endorsed or sponsored by Amtrak, not authorised representatives of Amtrak, and not an agent of Amtrak. A feed being published for anyone to download creates no relationship with the people who download it, and no relationship should be inferred from the fact that the times here are correct.

Why the word “Amtrak” appears on this site

Because it is the name of the thing being sold. A retailer selling travel on Amtrak trains has no way of saying what it sells without saying so. “Long-distance rail travel in the United States” does not tell a traveller which company's train will be at the platform, which fare rules apply, or whether a Roomette is on offer. Removing the word would not make the page more honest; it would make it unusable, and it would hide the one fact a buyer most needs.

Using someone else's trade mark to refer to their own product is ordinary and lawful, and it has a name: nominative fair use. In plain terms, you may use the mark to say whose product you are talking about, provided you use no more of it than the job requires and do not dress yourself up as the mark's owner. The test comes from New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, Inc., 971 F.2d 302 (9th Cir. 1992):

The three-part test

“First, the product or service in question must be one not readily identifiable without use of the trademark; second, only so much of the mark or marks may be used as is reasonably necessary to identify the product or service; and third, the user must do nothing that would, in conjunction with the mark, suggest sponsorship or endorsement by the trademark holder.”

New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, Inc., 971 F.2d 302 (9th Cir. 1992).

Applied to this site, each limb means something concrete. The first is satisfied by the subject matter: the routes, the station codes, the fare classes and the sleeping accommodations described here belong to one carrier's network and cannot be identified without naming it. The second sets the ceiling on how the name may appear, which is as a word in running text, set in the same typeface as every other word on the page. The third is not a one-off check but a standing constraint on the design, the wording and the branding, and it is the reason for the list in the next section.

One point of law is worth stating because it is often assumed to be otherwise: there is no US statute giving Amtrak special protection over its name. Ordinary trade mark law applies, and the same discipline would be owed to any other carrier's name.

What we do not do

Naming the carrier is permitted. Borrowing its identity is not, and the line between the two is drawn by the following, all of which are deliberate.

  • No Amtrak logo and no wordmark. Neither appears anywhere in this build, in any size, in any colour, including in a favicon, a payment badge or a footer.
  • No Amtrak colours. The palette a storefront uses is the agency's own, and none of the demonstration brands is dressed in the carrier's livery colours.
  • No copy of the carrier's website. The layout, the type, the station picker, the departure board and the wording were designed here. Nothing is reproduced from the carrier's site, and the site is not framed, embedded or mirrored.
  • No scraping of the carrier's site. Amtrak's Terms of Use prohibit using “any robot, spider, site search/retrieval application or other manual or automatic device or process to retrieve, index, ‘data mine’ or in any way reproduce or circumvent the navigational structure or presentation of the Site or its contents”. Our schedules come from the published GTFS feed, which exists to be downloaded.
  • No presenting ourselves as the carrier. No “official site”, no “official partner”, no implication that a booking is being made by the carrier or that a message comes from it. The agency's name leads every page, and the carrier is referred to in the third person throughout.

Consumer protection

A rail ticket has to come from the carrier or an authorised agent

This is the part of the page that matters most to somebody spending money, so it is put plainly and without softening. Amtrak's ticket Terms and Conditions state that Amtrak may refuse to carry passengers:

Amtrak ticket Terms and Conditions

“Who present an Amtrak ticket purchased from an unauthorized third party. Amtrak tickets may only be sold or issued by Amtrak or an authorized travel agent/tour operator. Any ticket purchased from an unauthorized third party will be voided. The ticket holder will not be eligible for travel or for a refund.”

Listed among the grounds on which Amtrak may refuse to carry a passenger.

The consequence falls on the traveller rather than on the seller. A ticket bought from an unauthorised source is not merely a bad deal; it is void, it does not entitle anyone to travel, and it does not entitle anyone to their money back. That is usually discovered on the day, at the platform, with luggage.

What to check before you pay

First, establish who you are buying from. Not the brand at the top of the page, but the legal entity that takes the payment and appears on the receipt, and where it is registered. A retailer that will not say clearly which company you are contracting with has answered the question.

Second, understand what authorisation actually is, because it is not a badge a website can award itself. Amtrak publishes no public fare or booking API, so live inventory reaches a reseller only through a distribution agreement: directly with Amtrak, through the agent portal now operated by Distribusion Technologies, or through an aggregator such as SilverRail. A seller with none of those has no route to the carrier's live inventory at all, whatever its site displays.

Third, look at the references you are given. An Amtrak reservation number is six alphanumeric characters, and the 13-digit ticket number appears on the sales receipt rather than on the eTicket itself. A document that only the seller can show you, carrying a reference of some other shape, is the failure worth catching a week early rather than at the gate.

An agency putting this platform into service has to hold a distribution agreement in its own name before it sells anything. That agreement, and nothing else, is the difference between an authorised retailer and an unauthorised third party. This demonstration holds none, which is why it issues nothing.

What this is, and what it is not

This site is a working demonstration of a white-label booking platform, built so that the interface, the accessibility work and the booking flow can be examined by people who assess such things for a living. The schedules in it are real. They are built from Amtrak's published GTFS feed, which holds 646 stops, 61 routes and 2,992 trips, with a service calendar running to 2 August 2027, so a search returns services that genuinely run.

Two limitations of that feed shape what you see. It contains no fare data of any kind, so every price shown here is generated by the demonstration and is not a quotation for anything. And Amtrak publishes no GTFS-Realtime feed, so nothing on this site knows whether a train is running late today.

We did correct the feed rather than ingest it blindly. Twelve timezone defects were found and fixed: seven Arizona stations were labelled America/Denver, though Arizona does not observe daylight saving, and five Gulf Coast stations were labelled America/New_York when Mississippi and Alabama are Central. Five of those also had their stop times written in local time rather than the feed's Eastern convention.

What this is not is a shop. No payment is taken, no card details are collected, nothing is sent to Amtrak or to any other carrier, and no reservation is created in anybody's system. A confirmation produced here is a screen in a demonstration, not a ticket, and it is not valid for travel. The rail industry has formal machinery for keeping that distinction watertight: the European standard for rail ticket barcodes, ERA TAP TSI Technical Document B.12, carries a specimen flag inside the barcode data, described as “The bit must be set if the barcode is issued for test purpose”, alongside a test-key regime under which “tickets signed using a test key can never be considered as valid”. The same idea is stated here in words: nothing this build produces is a ticket.

Do not book real travel here. To travel on a real service, buy from the carrier or from an authorised travel agent. If you have a question about this demonstration or about who operates this storefront, use the contact page.
